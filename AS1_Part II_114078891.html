Fitts' Law: Break-Reminder Selection

Nguyen Hong Chau Linh | 114078891

This assignment applies Fitts’ Law to a break-support interface for laptop work. The experiment tests how the distance and width of a “Break” target affect pointing time. It does not test whether users actually take more breaks or reduce sitting.

1. Scenario

Students and knowledge workers often spend extended periods reading, writing, analyzing information, and editing documents on laptops. During these working episodes, short breaks or micro-breaks may provide opportunities to recover from sustained effort and support well-being and performance (Lyubykh et al., 2022; Nie et al., 2023).

This assignment focuses on one small part of a break-support system: selecting the break action once it is available. Participants work in a simulated document interface where the destination is a single “Break” action.

The experiment evaluates pointing performance only. It does not show that a particular design makes people notice reminders more often, take more breaks, reduce sitting, or recover better.

2. Innovation & Application

Innovation

Instead of using abstract targets alone, the pointing task is embedded in a familiar document-work context. The fixed “Continue working” control establishes a standardized pointer location, while the “Break” button is the actual interface target that changes position and width between trials.

Minimal notification chrome is retained around “Break” to preserve the desktop-notification framing, but no heading, description, icon, countdown, or secondary action is shown. The short label also remains on one line across all three target widths, reducing text wrapping as a possible source of variation.

Application

The findings can inform the placement and size of break-support actions when the design goal is to make them easier to acquire with a pointer. A shorter movement distance or wider target should reduce pointing difficulty under Fitts’ Law, but this should be interpreted as an interaction-efficiency result rather than evidence of better break compliance.

Design intention Isolate the motor cost of reaching the break action while preserving a recognizable laptop-work context.
Control strategy Keep wording, color, button height, movement direction, document content, and starting location constant; vary only target distance and width.

3. Fitts’ Law Formula

SHANNON FORMULATION OF FITTS’ LAW
T = a + b log2 ( A W + 1 )
T
Movement time: the time from pressing “Continue working” until successfully pressing “Break.”
a
Regression intercept: the estimated baseline time when Index of Difficulty approaches zero.
b
Regression slope: the estimated increase in movement time for each additional bit of task difficulty.
A
Movement amplitude: the actual distance from the recorded starting pointer coordinates to the center of “Break.”
W
Target width in the direction of movement: the rendered horizontal width of “Break.”
log2(A/W + 1)
Index of Difficulty (ID), measured in bits. A farther target and/or a smaller target produces a larger ID.

Fitts (1954) introduced the original speed–accuracy relationship. The form log2(A/W + 1) is commonly called the Shannon formulation in HCI; MacKenzie (1992) is cited here for its use and treatment in Fitts’ Law research.

4. Method & Experimental Design

The experiment manipulates the two variables central to Fitts’ Law: movement amplitude and target width. “Continue working” serves only as the standardized starting target for the pointing task; it is not treated as a behavioral choice between continuing work and taking a break.

Design element Levels / rule Justification
Nominal distance 250, 450, 650 CSS px Creates near, medium, and far placements of the “Break” target while keeping all targets fully visible.
Target width 80, 130, 180 CSS px Provides three visually distinctive small, medium, and large button widths while remaining plausible for a desktop action.
Target height 48 CSS px, fixed Keeps the vertical hit area constant so the main size manipulation is horizontal width, matching the horizontal movement direction.
Conditions 3 distances × 3 widths = 9 Produces a useful range of Index of Difficulty values from relatively easy to relatively difficult conditions.
Repetitions 6 per condition = 54 main trials Repeated measurements reduce the influence of random trial-to-trial motor variability.
Practice 3 trials Lets participants learn the interaction before main data collection; practice is kept separate from the experimental dataset.

Trial procedure

“Continue working” and “Break” are both visible before the trial begins. Timing starts on pointerdown at “Continue working” and ends on a successful pointerdown inside “Break.” Timing uses performance.now().

The experiment records actual movement amplitude from the participant’s starting click coordinates to the rendered target center, and records the rendered target width rather than assuming the nominal value. Misses are counted while timing continues until a successful selection.

Main trials are randomized. If the page loses visibility or the browser is resized during an active trial, that attempt is recorded as interrupted and the same logical trial is repeated rather than silently discarded.

I chose these three distance and width levels to create a clear range of pointing difficulty while keeping every target visible in the laptop-sized interface. These values are not Fitts’ Law constants; they are design choices for this experiment so that I can compare easier and harder target selections without scaling or scrolling.

Interpretation of the parameters

The intercept, a = 496.31 ms, represents the predicted movement time when the Index of Difficulty is zero. Because zero difficulty is outside the observed range, this value should be interpreted as an extrapolated model baseline rather than a direct measurement of reaction time.

The slope, b = 156.28 ms/bit, indicates that each additional bit of difficulty was associated with an estimated 156.28 ms increase in average movement time. This slope is steeper than the 90.47 ms/bit obtained in Part I. The difference may reflect the different target ranges and contextual interface used in this task, as well as normal within-user variation such as practice, fatigue, or changes in speed–accuracy strategy. These are possible explanations only because they were not directly tested.

The model’s R² = 0.693 means that Index of Difficulty explained approximately 69.3% of the variation in the nine condition-average movement times.

Figure 1. Average movement time as a function of index of difficulty across the nine experimental conditions. The line shows the fitted linear regression.

Overall, the results follow the expected direction of Fitts’ Law: more difficult targets generally required longer movement times. However, the data come from one run of one user, so the fitted model should be interpreted within this limited context.

7. Limitations

The experiment isolates pointing performance in a simplified document-work scenario. A fixed starting location, one horizontal movement direction, and controlled target placements improve experimental consistency but do not reproduce the full variability of real knowledge work.

Another point I was thinking about is where the break button should appear on the screen. In this experiment, I only changed its horizontal distance from the starting point, mainly to keep the Fitts’ Law task simple and controlled. However, in a real interface, a break button may appear closer to the current work area, at the side of the screen, or in a corner. These positions may also affect how easy the button is to reach because they involve different movement directions and screen boundaries. I think this could be explored in future work as another design factor, while keeping target size and distance controlled.

The minimal notification shell is intentionally simplified. Real break-support systems may include more text, icons, timing logic, or a more stable notification location, while actual pointer positions, task urgency, attention, and notification timing vary naturally.

Therefore, the results should support claims about target acquisition efficiency only. Questions about real break-taking patterns, recovery, sitting reduction, or long-term reminder effectiveness require additional behavioral or field-based measures.
